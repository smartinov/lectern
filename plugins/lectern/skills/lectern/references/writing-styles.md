# Writing styles

These are original editorial briefs, expressed through reusable traits. Choose
one as the base and tune it with the learner. Do not use an author's name as a
generation instruction, reproduce signature passages, or invent lived experience.

All presets teach the same underlying facts and preserve the same learning
outcomes. Their differences concern how the reader encounters the explanation.
An entertaining phrase cannot substitute for the mechanism, evidence, or answer.

## Blunt Mentor (`blunt-mentor`)

Address the reader as a capable adult. Be candid about tempting shortcuts and
their consequences. Use concrete verbs, direct explanations, dry humor, and
occasional profanity when it adds emphasis. Direct criticism at faulty reasoning
or a frustrating situation, never the learner's intelligence or worth.

Develop an explanation in connected paragraphs. Vary sentence length. Let a
short sentence land after the reader has enough context. Avoid a stream of
one-liners, fake contrarianism, motivational slogans, and repeated rhetorical
formulas. Technical depth stays substantial; define terms without patronizing.

Sample — retrieval practice:

> You finish a chapter and everything makes sense. Excellent. Now close the
> book and explain the main idea. This is where the comfortable nodding often
> ends. While you were reading, the explanation was right there, doing much of
> the work. Recalling it yourself exposes the missing connections. Give it an
> honest attempt, check the answer, and repair the gap. Tomorrow, try again with
> a different example. The point is to be able to use the idea when the author
> is no longer whispering the answer in your ear.

## Case-Study Storyteller (`case-study-storyteller`)

Begin with a person facing a specific decision. Establish stakes quickly, then
follow choices and consequences through the concept being taught. Alternate
scene and explanation; make the causal mechanism explicit in the debrief.
Use one coherent case rather than several disposable anecdotes.

Label invented cases as hypothetical. Real cases need sources. Do not invent
quotes, claim personal experience, or add suspense by withholding a definition
the reader needs. Keep story details only when they clarify the decision.

Sample — the same concept:

> Imagine Maya preparing to teach a colleague a new process. She rereads her
> notes on Monday and feels ready. On Tuesday, her colleague asks why the second
> step matters. Maya remembers the diagram, but the explanation has disappeared.
> She returns to her notes, identifies the missing connection, and tries the
> explanation again without looking. On Thursday she practices with a different
> case. This time her preparation includes retrieving and using the idea, as
> well as reading about it. The question that interrupted her confidence has
> become a useful part of her study routine.

## Socratic Guide (`socratic-guide`)

Invite a prediction before explaining a mechanism. Reveal a useful counterexample,
ask what changed, and then resolve the question clearly. Use questions to focus
reasoning, not to imply the learner should already know the answer.

On Kindle, provide the complete explanation in the lesson and answers in the
appendix. Never require a live chat to understand the material. Limit successive
unanswered questions; the reader should not have to solve your prose.

Sample — the same concept:

> Suppose you can follow an explanation perfectly. Does that mean you could
> reconstruct it tomorrow? Try a small test: close the notes and explain why the
> first step leads to the second. What information was available while reading
> that is missing now? The notes supplied cues and connections; recalling the
> idea requires you to produce them. After an attempt, compare your explanation
> with the source and repair errors. Repeat after a delay with a new example.
> You are practicing the act you want to perform later: producing and applying
> the explanation without having it in front of you.

The samples apply the [retrieval and spacing evidence](learning-design.md).
They illustrate voice choices, not measured differences in learning effectiveness.

## Calibration and feedback

For a new course, present a 250–400 word sample from its actual subject, covering
an explanation and a concrete example. Keep scope, outline, and sample in one
review checkpoint. Ask for adjustments that matter: too abrasive, too slow,
too much setup, examples too distant, or insufficient technical depth.

Record the accepted sample and adjustments in the private outline. Store durable
preferences in the profile only when the learner states them or agrees to the
change. A difficult quiz is not evidence that the learner dislikes a voice.

Use the profile's `writing` fields to adjust humor, profanity, technical depth,
and narrative density. Course-specific changes belong in `personalization` in
the course specification. Avoid proliferating new presets for small adjustments.

After reading, separately ask about enjoyment, perceived clarity, and actual
elapsed time. Use quiz answers and later application to assess learning. Treat
a single learner's feedback as personal tuning, not a controlled experiment.
